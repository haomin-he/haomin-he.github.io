## Working Papers

**Belief Bias, Source Preference, and Preference for Robo-Advisor**<a href="/assets/Robo_Advisor.pdf" target="_blank" style="display: inline-block; padding: 1px 6px; margin-left: 10px; background-color: #f1f5f9; color: #334155; border: 1px solid #cbd5e1; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.75em; font-weight: 600; text-decoration: none; line-height: 1.2; vertical-align: middle; transition: all 0.2s;"><i class="fas fa-file-pdf" style="color: #ef4444; margin-right: 4px;"></i> PDF</a><br>
<span style="font-size: 0.9em;">with <a href="https://www.paulcheung.net/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Paul H. Y. Cheung</a> and <a href="https://likklab.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">King King Li</a></span><br>
<em style="font-size: 0.9em;">Outstanding Paper Award of the 7th China Behavioral and Experimental Economics Forum</em>

<details style="margin-bottom: 5px;">
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <div style="font-size: 0.9em; color: #555; line-height: 1.6; padding-left: 15px; border-left: 3px solid #e0e0e0; margin-top: 10px;">
    Do people prefer robo-advisors because they believe the algorithm is more accurate, or because they favor it independent of performance? This paper investigates preferences for human versus robo-advisors using a dynamic source preference framework that allows for belief bias. We use a two-part experiment in which advice is generated by real-life investors and ChatGPT, and subjects value advice before and after learning advisors' objective accuracy. This design enables a dynamic decomposition of belief bias from source preference. Before disclosure, subjects are more likely to prefer robo-advice over human advice. After learning that the two advisors perform similarly, many subjects revise their valuations away from the robo-advisor and toward the human advisor, indicating that initial robo-preference partly reflects biased beliefs about algorithmic competence. Yet, a substantial subset continues to prefer the robo-advisor after disclosure, revealing source preference independent of performance. We also find that subjects have a stronger preference for robo-advisor under uncertainty than under risk. The preference for robo-advisor is reduced significantly after the uncertainty on performance is resoled via the disclosure of accuracy information, supporting the competence hypothesis of Heath and Tversky (1991). Overall, the results show that belief bias and source preference jointly shape demand for robo-advice, and the preference for robo-advisor was stronger under uncertainty.
  </div>
</details>
<div style="font-size: 0.9em; color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 25px;">Presentations (selected): D-TEA 2026*, FUR Conference 2026*, 7th China Behavioral and Experimental Economics Forum (2025), SZU Public Policy and Experimental Economics Workshop 2023.</div>

**Illusion on Causality: An Experimental Study**<br>
<span style="font-size: 0.9em;">with <a href="https://likklab.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">King King Li</a></span><br>
<em style="font-size: 0.9em;">Reject & Resubmit, Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization</em>

<details style="margin-bottom: 5px;">
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <div style="font-size: 0.9em; color: #555; line-height: 1.6; padding-left: 15px; border-left: 3px solid #e0e0e0; margin-top: 10px;">
    This paper experimentally investigates whether individuals exhibit an illusion regarding the causal relationship between moral behavior and investment returns, i.e., mistakenly believing that there is a causality. We find a positive correlation between the amount subjects sent in the dictator game and their willingness to pay for risky investments, but only when the dictator game precedes the investment decision; this relationship is not observed when the dictator game follows the investment decision. This pattern is strongest for sin stocks, and we find that subjects who gave relatively little show significantly depressed valuations for sin stocks compared to subjects who never faced the dictator game at all, while generous givers do not differ from this baseline. This pattern cannot be explained by a wide range of theories, including altruism, signaling, moral licensing, image concern, or a stable individual trait, since such accounts do not predict that the relationship should depend on whether giving precedes or follows the investment decision. We do not find evidence that giving in the dictator game is associated with subjects' explicit beliefs about their chance of winning, suggesting that the illusion on causality operates on subjects' valuations without a detectable shift in their stated probability beliefs.
  </div>
</details>
<div style="font-size: 0.9em; color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 25px;">Presentations (selected): HKUST*, 2024 China BEEF International Workshop.</div>

**Pricing for Product Awareness in Social Network: An Experimental Study**<br>
<span style="font-size: 0.9em;">with <a href="https://likklab.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">King King Li</a> and <a href="https://yujingxu.weebly.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Yujing Xu</a></span>

<details style="margin-bottom: 5px;">
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <div style="font-size: 0.9em; color: #555; line-height: 1.6; padding-left: 15px; border-left: 3px solid #e0e0e0; margin-top: 10px;">
    We experimentally investigate the impact of social networks on pricing competition by comparing outcomes under various levels of network connectivity against a no-network benchmark. In the experiment, some consumers are aware of the product and some are not. When there is social network, consumers who are not aware of the product can learn about it from their friends who purchased it earlier. In such case, firms may be motivated to set lower price to induce consumers to purchase so that their unaware friends can be aware of the product in later stage and potentially purchase the product. The experimental result supports this hypothesis. The results suggest that price in the social network was lower than the price when there was no social network, and for a given social network, price increases in the proportion of consumers initially aware of the product. There was a non-monotone U-shaped relationship between the size of social network and price. We also report several interesting deviations from the theory.
  </div>
</details>
<div style="font-size: 0.9em; color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 25px;">Presentations (selected): 2nd East Asia Experimental and Behavioral Economics Workshop*, Shanghai Jiaotong University*.</div>
